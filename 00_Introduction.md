When we understand something, we say: "_I see_".

When someone plans something out for the long-term, we say that they have a "_vision_".

Sometimes we even call people like that a "_visionary_".

The English language features many such examples of using the phenomenon of vision (seeing) as a metaphor. And this seems hardly surprising given how much of our regular experience is dominated by the visual sense. Out of all our senses, vision also takes up the largest fraction of our brains. In the macaque monkey, the primate whose visual system we know best, more than half of the neocortex is devoted to vision ([](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/1.1.1)).

Vision is not essential for survival since blind people live their lives just fine. But even blind people seem to make use of their visual system, such as using vision-dedicated brain areas to process touch or spatial relations more generally. In people who are blind from early in life, reading Braille activates the primary visual cortex, the part of the brain that in sighted people receives the signals from the eyes ([](https://doi.org/10.1038/380526a0)).

So, what is that, _seeing_? What do we know about vision?

This textbook aims to provide both a broad overview on the scientific study of vision as well as insights into cutting edge developments. The main focus are **biological visual systems**, but we will also touch on **artificial visual systems** in the form of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI). More specifically, we will discuss fundamental insights into the visual system of mammals, with particular emphasis on the visual system of primates, and hence humans. 

Vision is tightly linked to the physics of light (aka optics), so we will start by reviewing basic concepts of how light arises, spreads, and interacts with the environment. These insights will explain the optical apparatus of the eye, such as the lens. In turn, this will lead us to examine the anatomy of the eye. Which, in turn, will lead us to discuss the basic function of the eye, including the neuronal circuit that is embedded within. Much of what follows after that is a systematic survey of what we know about the various interconnected neuronal circuits that receive the neuronal signals from the eye. Since computational models in the form of artificial neural networks (ANNs) are inspired by these insights, we will discuss their development as well.

In other words, much of this textbook is focused on the **neuroscience of vision**, with a deeper premise that neuronal interactions are at the heart of scientific descriptions of visual function and the phenomenon of "seeing".

Lastly, we will ponder about the difference between unconscious **visual processing** and the related, but separate phenomenon of **visual experience**. Science is rapidly advancing on both these fronts in that we are rapidly uncovering new knowledge about how causal interactions between neurons support both sensing the visual environment in order to guide behavior and experiencing the visual environment in that we do not just react to what is in front of our eyes akin to a self-driving car, but also consciously _see_ things such as the redness of red and the greenness of green (and sometimes even do so without any light entering our eyes).
